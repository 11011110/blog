* [The $$13\times 13$$ zebra graph is untraceable](https://garden.meghanto.me/notes/zebra-graph/) <span style="white-space:nowrap">([$$\mathbb{M}$$](https://mathstodon.xyz/@meghanto/117383822049339264)).</span> A zebra is a fairy chess piece that jumps like a longer knight, two steps in one direction and three in another. * The [Harvard/Smithsonian Astrophysics Data System](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/), a large bibliographic database for astronomy, astrophysics, and related fields, has been transitioning for the last year or so to become [SciX](https://scixplorer.org/search) with expanded areas of coverage "across space, Earth, and planetary sciences", and is scheduled to complete the move in mid-November <span style="white-space:nowrap">([$$\mathbb{M}$$](https://mathstodon.xyz/@11011110/117390703766499631)).</span> It's difficult to find much about the move from adsabs to SciX that isn't both heavy on promotion and light on content, but [their press release](https://www.cfa.harvard.edu/news/new-nasa-backed-research-platform-scix-expands-open-science) is better than some, and there's [documentation on how to use SciX](https://scixplorer.org/scixhelp/).

  If you've ever wondered what those "bibcode" tags associated with some scientific references are, [they're pointers into this database](https://scixplorer.org/scixhelp/actions-scix/bibcode). I imagine non-mathematicians have similar questions about what those "MR" and "Zbl" tags used for mathematical references are; the answer is the same, pointers to the MathSciNet and zbMATH databases respectively. And now I'm wondering why DBLP doesn't have its own tags for computer science references.

* If your $$\LaTeX$$ input encodes an en-dash as a double-hyphen `--`, TeX will allow a line break after it. If it encodes it as a unicode en-dash character `–`, it will not allow a line break, both in pdflatex with `\usepackage[utf8]{inputenc}` and lualatex without <span style="white-space:nowrap">([$$\mathbb{M}$$](https://mathstodon.xyz/@11011110/117402476663719722)).</span>  Additionally, with the unicode en-dashes, pdflatex and lualatex will differ in how they break the words between the dashes: pdflatex will not break and hyphenate "Koebe–Andreev–Thurston" at all, but lualatex will break it as "Koebe–An- [line break] dreev–Thurston". With double-hyphens, both are happy to produce the much better output "Koebe– [line break] Andreev–Thurston". So let this be a lesson not to try to be fancy in how you type your dashes in $$\LaTeX$$. Just use double hyphens (or triple hyphens for em-dashes) and let $$\LaTeX$$ do the conversion to dashes.

* [On searching Wikipedia for article titles containing colons, like "ST:TNG"](https://hachyderm.io/@simontatham/117180445505761503). It will think you are looking for an article titled "TNG" in the Sotho-language Wikipedia, regardless of how you try to escape the colon. But it turns out that, for Wikipedia, colons that are actually given in titles rather than used to demark other namespaces are equivalent to spaces. So the string you want to search for is "ST TNG".

* [Numberphile video on Connor Hill's classification of noble polyhedra](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=95335U-cUh8) <span style="white-space:nowrap">([$$\mathbb{M}$$](https://mathstodon.xyz/@richardelwes/117371098364236341)).</span> A [noble polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_polyhedron) has all faces and all vertices symmetric to each other, but not necessarily all edges. There are two infinite families and a lot of sporadic examples, especially if you allow self-intersections. Hill won the 2026 [International Science and Engineering Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Science_and_Engineering_Fair), a high school student competition, for his determination of the complete list.
